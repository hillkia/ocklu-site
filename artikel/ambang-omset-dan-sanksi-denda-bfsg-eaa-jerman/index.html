Micro-Enterprise Exemptions and Turnover Thresholds Under the BFSG

Under the German Barrierefreiheitsstärkungsgesetz (BFSG)—which transposes the European Accessibility Act (EAA)—micro-enterprises offering services are explicitly exempt from statutory accessibility obligations [16]. To qualify for this exemption, a business must satisfy two cumulative statutory limits: it must employ fewer than 10 workers and record a maximum annual turnover of €2 million [4, 16].

However, merchants frequently misinterpret the scope of this provision, assuming a blanket defense against all regulatory exposure. This assumption collapses when businesses fail to distinguish between products and services. While service-providing micro-enterprises enjoy exemptions, economic operators bringing physical products into circulation face stringent compliance mandates regardless of their size [16]. Furthermore, merchants overlooking cross-border turnover limits—failing to aggregate European-wide transactions toward the €2 million threshold—expose themselves to heavy regulatory penalties [4, 16].

When violations occur, enforcement authorities can impose severe administrative fines (Bußgeld) ranging from €10,000 up to €100,000, determined by the severity of the violation, the extent of the accessibility failure, and the organization's responsiveness [18]. Beyond direct regulatory fines, non-compliant businesses face dual enforcement risks, including potential civil claims and warning letters from competitors under unfair competition laws [18].

Enforcement Mechanisms, Penalties, and Legal Uncertainty

Under the German Barrierefreiheitsstärkungsgesetz (BFSG), enforcement is structured around administrative oversight and punitive measures [18]. Market surveillance authorities possess the power to issue corrective orders, restrict or prohibit the offering of non-compliant services, and impose administrative fines (Bußgeld) [18, 2]. Statutory references concerning these enforcement powers, including § 2, § 3, and § 14, outline the framework for compliance, while § 17 addresses exemptions such as disproportionate burdens [12, 13]. When violations occur regarding service provisions, documentation, or CE markings, fines can range from EUR 10,000 to EUR 100,000, scaled according to the severity, widespread nature of the accessibility failure, and organizational response [18].

Despite these clear penalty brackets, significant enforcement gaps persist. Current regulatory sources lack verified audit data for micro-enterprises, making it difficult to assess baseline compliance across smaller market actors. Compliance auditors face severe operational challenges when navigating vague legislative mandates, as ambiguous statutory language leaves room for interpretive drift. On top of official regulatory oversight, businesses face a dual enforcement risk: competitor warning letters (Abmahnung) and civil claims under unfair competition law [18].

Unresolved Conflicts: Compliance Costs versus Digital Inclusion Rights

The implementation of the Barrierefreiheitsstärkungsgesetz (BFSG) and the European Accessibility Act (EAA) exposes a profound ideological and economic collision between digital commerce operators and disability rights advocates [16, 18]. On one side, e-commerce merchants—particularly smaller online retailers operating on tight margins—face severe regulatory pressures, including administrative fines ranging from €10,000 to €100,000 for non-compliance, alongside the constant threat of civil warning letters from competitors under unfair competition laws [18]. Merchants argue that achieving complete compliance demands extensive technical overhauls of digital platforms, which places an unsustainable financial burden on operations that already navigate strict market surveillance [17, 18].

Conversely, disability rights advocates demand absolute and uncompromised digital inclusion, asserting that web environments, online shops, and subscription models must be fully accessible to guarantee equal participation for people with disabilities under EU law [16, 18]. The fundamental friction lies in the reality that advocacy groups push for immediate, universal accessibility without adequately acknowledging the prohibitive costs, technical complexities, and lack of standardized audit frameworks for businesses operating outside large corporate structures [18]. While the legislation provides narrow exemptions for micro-enterprises under specific service thresholds, merchants bringing products into circulation face strict liabilities regardless of scale [17]. This leaves a contested gray area where advocates view financial pushback as an evasion of civil rights, while merchants perceive rigid accessibility mandates as an existential threat to their profit margins.
