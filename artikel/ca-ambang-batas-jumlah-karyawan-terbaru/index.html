The 100‑Employee Trigger: Pay Data Reporting

California law obligates any private employer that employs 100 or more workers—including those hired through labor contractors—to submit an annual pay‑data report to the California Civil Rights Department (CRD) [5][6]. The report must detail compensation, gender, race/ethnicity, and job‑category information for all California employees and, when applicable, a separate report for the contractor‑supplied workforce [5][6].

The statutory authority for this requirement is Senate Bill 1162, enacted on September 27 2022 and codified at Government Code § 12999 and Labor Code § 432.3 [6]. SB 1162 expands the earlier SB 973 framework by broadening coverage to labor‑contractor workers and by adding new data fields such as remote‑worker counts [5].

The rule became effective on January 1 2023; the first filing deadline for the 2023 reporting year is May 8 2024 [5]. Employers falling below the 100‑employee threshold are exempt from the mandatory filing, although they may still be subject to other pay‑transparency obligations [6].

In summary, the 100‑employee threshold activates a mandatory, CRD‑filed pay‑data reporting duty grounded in SB 1162, covering both direct hires and labor‑contractor workers, with compliance required beginning January 1 2023.

The 75-Employee Trigger: WARN Act Notices

The California WARN Act obligates covered employers to provide 60 days’ advance notice to affected employees, their representatives, and local government agencies before a plant closing or a mass layoff — the same notice period required by the federal WARN statute — but it applies to a smaller workforce size [18]. Under the revised rule, the trigger is 75 or more employees (instead of the previous 100‑employee benchmark), and a layoff of 50 employees qualifies as a “mass layoff” regardless of the percentage of the total workforce [18].

The legal foundation for this requirement is California Government Code § 3132, commonly referred to as the California WARN Act. The amendment that lowered the threshold from 100 to 75 employees was enacted to align state protections with the realities of smaller employers and to close a gap where firms just under the former limit could avoid notice obligations [18].

Practical implication: By expanding coverage to businesses that employ as few as 75 workers, a larger segment of small‑to‑medium enterprises must now develop layoff‑notification procedures, maintain accurate head‑count records, and coordinate with local agencies. Companies that previously considered themselves exempt may face compliance costs, potential penalties for non‑notice, and the need to revise internal HR policies to meet the new statutory deadline. [18]
