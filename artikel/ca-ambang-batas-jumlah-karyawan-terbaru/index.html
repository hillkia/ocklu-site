Labor Law Obligations Triggered by Scale

California’s employment landscape is defined by specific numerical thresholds that trigger distinct legal obligations, creating a tiered compliance structure for growing businesses. The most fundamental distinction is not a single employee count, but rather the classification of the worker itself. Under the "ABC test," codified in Labor Code sections 2775-2787 via AB 5 and AB 2257, a worker is presumed an employee unless specific criteria are met [13]. This classification determines whether wage and hour protections apply, as these provisions generally cover any business with one or more employees [1].

As the workforce expands, anti-discrimination and leave requirements escalate in complexity. The Fair Employment and Housing Act (FEHA) mandates that employers regularly employing five or more employees must adhere to anti-discrimination provisions and provide up to six weeks of leave for normal pregnancy or four months for pregnancy disability [1]. Sexual harassment protections under FEHA apply to any business with at least one employee [1]. At the fifteen-employee mark, federal Title VII of the Civil Rights Act becomes applicable, adding a layer of federal anti-discrimination compliance [1].

Regarding unionization, the National Labor Relations Act (NLRA) governs collective bargaining and union relationships. Contrary to the common assumption that union rights activate only at larger scales, the NLRA applies to any business with one or more employees [1]. This means the legal framework for concerted activity and union organizing is present from the very first hire, although practical enforcement and organizational dynamics often shift as the company grows.

Mandatory benefits also trigger at specific counts. For instance, California Health & Safety Code Section 1366.21 ("Cal-COBRA") applies to businesses with two or more employees [1]. Additionally, paid family temporary disability insurance leave under SB 1661 applies to any business with one or more employees [1].
